    Programming the H100¶

    A hardware-level companion guide to NVIDIA Hopper¶

    The gap between "my kernel runs" and "my kernel saturates the tensor cores" is exactly the size of this book. Thirteen chapters that start at the H100 die and end with a profiler telling you which of your assumptions was wrong — every one with ASCII diagrams, worked examples with concrete numbers, and code you can read line by line.

    Verified on: CUDA 12.x + Hopper GPU (sm_90a). Compile CUDA examples with: nvcc -std=c++17 -arch=sm_90a -O2 <file>.cu -o <out> Run with: ./<out> on any H100 or B200 instance.

    How to read this¶

    The chapters build on each other. Chapter 1 is pure architecture — no code, just the machine. Chapters 2–3 give you the instruction-level vocabulary (clusters, inline PTX, state spaces, mbarrier). Chapters 4–5 are about getting bytes into shared memory efficiently. Chapters 6–7 are the tensor cores. Chapters 8–10 put it together into real warp-specialized kernels. Chapter 11 leaves the single GPU behind. Chapters 12–13 are the payoff: one production kernel that uses everything at once, and the tool that measures it.

    If you already write CUDA and want the Hopper-specific parts, start at Chapter 2. If you are here for GEMM, read 4 → 5 → 6 → 7 → 8 → 9 in order. If you have a slow kernel in front of you right now, start at Chapter 13.

    Contents¶

    • Part I — The Machine

      1. NVIDIA H100 GPU Architecture

      The TMA unit, asynchronous execution, 4th-gen Tensor Cores, the memory hierarchy from HBM3 through L2/L1 to registers, SM microarchitecture (quadrants, warp schedulers, dispatch units, SFUs, LSUs), NVLink 4.0, shared memory banking and swizzling, and how thread blocks are distributed across SMs.

    • Part II — The Instruction Layer

      2. Clusters, Inline PTX, Data Types & Pointers

      Thread block clusters, distributed shared memory, TMA multicast, the PTX ISA, inline asm() syntax with operands/constraints/clobbers, state spaces, deep-learning data types, byte addressing, shared memory banks, cvta and mapa.

      3. Asynchronicity, Barriers & the mbarrier Pipeline

      Latency hiding, blocking vs non-blocking operations, the Generic vs Async proxy memory model, RAW/WAR hazards, fences, the mbarrier primitive end to end, phase flipping, barrier.cluster, commit/wait groups, and named barriers.

    • Part III — Moving Data

      4. cuTensorMap, TMA & Swizzling

      What TMA is and why it exists, how descriptors replace manual address math, all 12 parameters of cuTensorMapEncodeTiled(), tensor rank, strides and box dimensions, 32B/64B/128B swizzling with the XOR formula, interleaving, L2 promotion, and out-of-bounds fill.

      5. cp.async.bulk, TMA Transfers & L2 Cache Hinting

      The cp.async.bulk family vs Ampere's cp.async, tensor-aware vs raw copies, every state-space combination, mbarrier vs bulk-group completion, L2 eviction hints and createpolicy, TMA multicast and the ctaMask, prefetching, and cp.reduce.async.

    • Part IV — Tensor Cores

      6. WGMMA — Warp Group Matrix Multiply-Accumulate

      The shift from warp to warp group, why wgmma.mma_async is non-blocking, the fence → load → compute → collect pipeline, every qualifier, operand location rules, ldmatrix and swizzle addressing, the 64-bit WGMMA descriptor field by field, and all supported tile shapes.

      7. WGMMA Part 2 — Groups, stmatrix, FP8 & Sparse

      Commit/wait group mechanics and what actually overlaps, the dual role of wgmma.fence, stmatrix writeback, the FP8 paradigm shift (E4M3 vs E5M2, scaling, cvt.satfinite, K-major layout, precision pitfalls), and sparse WGMMA with 2:4 structured sparsity.

    • Part V — Kernel Design

      8. Kernel Design, Warp Specialization & Pipelining

      The roofline model and arithmetic intensity, classifying compute-bound vs memory-bound kernels, producer/consumer warp specialization, setmaxnreg dynamic register allocation, warp group count selection, and circular multi-stage buffering.

      9. Pipelines, Epilogues & Persistent Scheduling

      The two-barrier handshake, ABA ambiguity and phase bits, full producer and consumer flows, cooperative vs ping-pong pipeline architectures, the six-stage epilogue fusion lifecycle, persistent scheduling, and rasterization for L2 locality.

      10. Stream-K Scheduling & Multi-Kernel Launch

      Stream-K work units and splits, the first/middle/final triplet, the lock array, deterministic vs non-deterministic reduction, HyTiS, the stream serialization utilization gap, GDC hardware grid handoff, programmatic dependent launch, and tuning the overlap window.

    • Part VI — Scaling Out

      11. Multi-GPU Systems, NCCL & Distributed Training

      NVLink and NVSwitch topology, the DGX H100 node, ConnectX-7 and the inter-node bottleneck, the SuperPOD, rail networks and SHARP, InfiniBand leaf/spine/core, UVA and peer access, SLURM and PMIx, NCCL and the big six collectives, and data / tensor / pipeline / expert parallelism.

    • Part VII — Putting It to Work

      12. FlashAttention-3 on the H100

      Why attention is memory-bound and how fusing it crosses the ridge point; the online softmax recurrence worked by hand; then the three Hopper techniques — producer/consumer warp specialization, ping-pong scheduling, and 2-stage intra-warpgroup pipelining — that take it from 35% to 75% of peak. Causal masking and load balance, and the FP8 path: block quantization, the K-major transpose of V, and Hadamard incoherent processing.

      13. Profiling with Nsight Compute

      Getting ncu to run, including the permission error that stops most first attempts; replay and what it does to your program; Speed of Light triage; the roofline chart and which ceiling actually applies on Hopper; sectors per request and bank conflicts; every warp stall reason mapped to the chapter that explains it; occupancy and why chasing it is wrong; the metric naming scheme; SASS and stall sampling; and four worked diagnoses from symptom to fix.

    Running this book locally¶

    pip install -r requirements.txt
mkdocs serve

    Then open http://127.0.0.1:8000.

    To publish it to GitHub Pages, push to main — the workflow in .github/workflows/ci.yml builds the site and deploys it for you. Edit the site_url and repo_url lines at the top of mkdocs.yml to point at your own repository first.
